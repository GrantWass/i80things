Every winter, the same scene repeats on I-80: a line of cars idles at a closed ramp, a driver decides the closure cannot really mean them, and a tow truck spends the next hour pulling them out of a drift.

Nebraska's interstate closures are not arbitrary. They happen when plows cannot keep a lane open, when visibility drops to nothing, or when abandoned vehicles make the road unsafe for everyone. Understanding how the decision gets made — and what to do when it does — is the difference between a delay and an emergency.

## Who closes I-80

The Nebraska Department of Transportation maintains the road and recommends closures. The Nebraska State Patrol enforces them. A closure can be segment-by-segment: westbound may close while eastbound stays open, or a 30-mile stretch may close while the rest of the corridor is fine.

There is no single "I-80 is closed" switch. That is why a statewide headline is almost always wrong. Check the specific segment.

## The three signals to watch

**1. NWS warnings.** A winter storm warning, blizzard warning or high wind warning is the advance notice. The National Weather Service offices in [Omaha/Valley](https://www.weather.gov/oax/), [Hastings](https://www.weather.gov/gid/), [North Platte](https://www.weather.gov/lbf/) and [Cheyenne](https://www.weather.gov/cys/) issue them by county and include timing. A warning does not mean the road will close, but it tells you when the window is.

**2. Nebraska 511.** Once conditions deteriorate, 511 is the official status. Segments are rated dry, wet, partially covered, covered or closed. The map also shows incidents, crashes and stalled vehicles — the things that actually force a closure.

**3. The plow tracker.** [plows.nebraska.gov](https://plows.nebraska.gov/) shows where NDOT equipment is working. If plows are parked, that is a signal. If they are running a route, the road may be passable behind them — but not necessarily at highway speed.

## Why the west closes first

The stretch from North Platte through Ogallala, Sidney and Kimball is high, open and windy. There is little to block the wind, and drifting snow can bury a lane in minutes. The same storm that produces a wet drive in Omaha can produce zero visibility at mile 126.

The corridor also has long gaps between services. A driver stranded west of Ogallala may be 30 miles from the nearest town with a motel. That is why NDOT closes earlier there and why the State Patrol is less patient with drivers who ignore barricades.

## If the gates are down

1. **Stop at the nearest town.** Do not park on the shoulder or the ramp. Find a truck stop, motel or open business.
2. **Do not take a county road.** Local roads are narrower, less maintained and not designed for interstate traffic. GPS will happily route you into a drift.
3. **Tell someone where you are.** If you are stuck, call 911 only for an emergency. For non-emergency road help, the State Patrol's \*55 line connects you to the nearest dispatch.
4. **Stay with your vehicle if you are stranded.** Run the engine intermittently for heat, keep the exhaust clear of snow, and leave a window cracked. Do not walk into a whiteout looking for help.

## The winter kit that actually matters

- A full tank of fuel before the western stretch
- Blankets, warm clothes and boots — not just a coat
- Water and food that will not freeze
- A phone charger and a backup battery
- A shovel, sand or kitty litter, and a flashlight
- Jumper cables and a tow strap
- A paper map, because cell coverage has gaps

## Plow etiquette

Do not pass a plow on the right. Do not tailgate one. The driver cannot see you in the spray, and the blade can throw debris. If you must pass, do it on the left when the lane is clear and the plow has moved over. Give the truck room — it is wider than it looks.

## The bottom line

A winter I-80 drive is a series of decisions, not one. Check the forecast the night before. Check 511 before you leave. Check it again at Grand Island or North Platte. If the road is closed, the safest and fastest option is almost always to wait.
