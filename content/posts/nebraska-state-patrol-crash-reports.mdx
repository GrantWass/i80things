A crash on I-80 produces two very different paper trails: a short public statement from the Nebraska State Patrol, and a formal crash report that lives with NDOT Records. Knowing which one you need saves a lot of wasted time.

## What the State Patrol publishes

The patrol's [newsroom](https://statepatrol.nebraska.gov/divisions/office-superintendent/news) posts releases on fatal crashes, serious injury crashes, pursuits, arrests and notable enforcement activity. Releases usually include:

- The date, time and approximate location (often a mile marker)
- The vehicles involved, without full identifying details in some cases
- Injury status and whether seat belts or alcohol were factors, when confirmed
- The investigating troop or office
- Charges, if any

What it does **not** publish: every minor collision, every disabled vehicle, or a running log of scanner traffic. A two-car fender-bender with no injuries may never appear.

That is why the patrol's releases are a starting point, not a comprehensive crash log. For corridor-wide trends, NDOT's [crash data pages](https://dot.nebraska.gov/safety/crash/) publish annual facts, fatality reports and monthly summaries.

## What is in a crash report

Nebraska crash reports are completed by the investigating agency and filed with NDOT. They typically contain:

- Exact location, including mile marker, roadway and weather conditions
- Driver and vehicle information
- Injuries and citations
- A narrative of what happened
- Witness information, when collected

They do **not** contain medical records or the identities of juveniles in most circumstances. Victim names may be withheld until family notification is complete.

## How to request one

The patrol's [crash report request page](https://statepatrol.nebraska.gov/services/crashaccident-report-requests) redirects to NDOT Records. The practical steps:

1. Identify the crash by date, location and agency. A mile marker and approximate time are enough to start.
2. Submit a records request through NDOT's crash reporting portal or call NDOT Records at **402-479-4645**.
3. Expect a fee. Nebraska charges for copies and research time; the amount depends on the request.
4. Expect a wait. This is a records process, not a same-day newsroom service. For breaking stories, rely on the patrol's release and on-scene reporting.

For fatal crashes, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's [FARS database](https://www.nhtsa.gov/research-data/fatality-analysis-reporting-system-fars) is the long-run dataset, but it lags by a year or more. It is useful for context, never for a breaking story.

## Why we do not publish scanner traffic as fact

Scanner feeds are how a lot of local news gets its first hint of a crash. They are also how a lot of local news gets it wrong. A dispatcher may say "possible ejection" before anyone has reached the vehicle. A caller may report the wrong direction. A pursuit may end three exits from where it started.

Our rule is simple: **a scanner call is a tip**. We confirm with the State Patrol, the county sheriff, NDOT's 511 incident layer, or an on-scene official before we publish a location, injury description or cause. We do not rebroadcast audio, and we do not publish tactical movements or unconfirmed victim information.

That standard is also why our [source directory](/sources) labels scanner feeds as community sources, not official ones — even when the feed itself is hosted by an agency.

## The fastest legitimate workflow

1. Monitor the [NSP newsroom](https://statepatrol.nebraska.gov/divisions/office-superintendent/news), [NSP on X](https://x.com/NEStatePatrol), and the [Nebraska 511](https://511.nebraska.gov/) incident layer.
2. If a scanner call sounds significant, note the time, mile marker and direction — then stop.
3. Call the patrol's public information office or the county dispatch supervisor. Ask what has been confirmed.
4. Check NDOT's 511 entry and any camera near the scene.
5. Publish only what you can attribute, and link to the primary source.
