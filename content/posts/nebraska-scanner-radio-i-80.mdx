Nebraska's I-80 corridor is covered by a patchwork of radio systems: State Patrol troops on the statewide system, city police and fire departments on local trunked systems, county sheriffs on their own channels, and NDOT maintenance crews on districts that span multiple counties. Most of it is unencrypted, and a meaningful share is streamed online.

That makes scanner traffic the fastest informal tip line in the state. It also makes it dangerous for anyone who treats a dispatcher's first sentence as a confirmed fact.

## Who is on which channel

- **Nebraska State Patrol** operates by troop. Troop A covers the Omaha area, Troop H the Lincoln area, and troops B through E cover the rest of the state. The statewide radio system carries their traffic; a single Broadcastify feed may cover only part of a troop.
- **Omaha and Lincoln** run their own trunked systems with separate police and fire talkgroups. Scanner apps split these into precinct or channel feeds.
- **County sheriffs** handle rural crashes and assist municipal departments. Their traffic is often the best source for a crash outside city limits.
- **NDOT districts** coordinate plows, closures and maintenance. During storms, their chatter can reveal a closure before it appears on 511.
- **Fire and EMS** agencies use their own dispatch channels. Medical details are usually restricted and should never be published from a scanner.

## What Nebraska law says

Nebraska is a one-party-consent state for recording conversations. Its interception statute, [Neb. Rev. Stat. § 86-290](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=86-290), expressly excludes radio communications that are "readily accessible to the general public," including governmental, law-enforcement, police and fire broadcasts, from the definition of interception.

In plain terms: listening to an unencrypted public-safety feed is legal in Nebraska. The federal framework has a similar public-safety carve-out.

That does **not** mean everything is fair game. Publishing material you unlawfully intercepted is a separate offense. And the legal right to listen does not create a legal right to rebroadcast someone else's stream, to publish tactical details, or to identify victims before authorities have notified families.

## How we use scanners

Our newsroom policy is deliberately conservative:

1. **A scanner call is a tip, never a source.** We do not publish a crash, a pursuit, an injury or a suspect description based on radio traffic alone.
2. **We confirm with the agency.** That means the State Patrol public information office, the county sheriff, the city police department or the incident commander. If nobody confirms it, it does not run.
3. **We do not publish tactical movements.** No staging locations, no officer positions, no "suspect last seen" details that could compromise an active response.
4. **We do not name victims before notification.** Even when a name is broadcast, we wait for an official release or a public family statement.
5. **We do not rebroadcast audio.** We link to the public feed and describe what we confirmed. We do not host clips.
6. **We respect delays.** Some agencies, including Council Bluffs, intentionally delay their public streams for officer safety. We do not try to defeat that.

## The practical feed list

Our [source directory](/sources) lists the feeds we monitor by stretch, including the State Patrol, Omaha, Lincoln, Sarpy, Douglas, Seward, Buffalo County/Kearney, Grand Island, Lincoln County/North Platte, Keith County/Ogallala, Cheyenne County/Sidney and the Council Bluffs border feeds.

A few practical notes:

- Broadcastify now requires a **free account** to listen live. Archives and the Calls API are paid.
- Some agency feeds are official; many are hosted by volunteers. Labeling matters.
- OpenMHz carries some Nebraska systems but has no public API and blocks automated access.
- Coverage gaps are real. There is no dedicated feed for every county, and Grand Island's digital police traffic is not fully carried on its public feed.

## Why this matters for a news site

A local news operation that publishes scanner rumors destroys trust quickly. A local news operation that waits 20 minutes for confirmation gets the story right. On a corridor where a wrong mile marker can send someone the wrong direction, the second approach is the only one worth having.
