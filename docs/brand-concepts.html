Three ways to put Nebraska in the mark

All three keep the interstate badge, the green, the amber and the serif wordmark. The difference is how loud the Nebraska reference is. Judged at 16 px first: if it turns to mush there, it is decoration, not a mark.

1. Kernel Dot

A single amber corn kernel replaces the dot over the “i” in Things, and the full stop at the end. The badge does not change at all. At a glance it reads as punctuation; look closer and it is a kernel.

Where it livesWordmark, footer, OG cards, Instagram caption watermark. Badge stays as-is.
Why it worksZero legibility risk. Nobody notices until they do, which is the definition of subtle.
RiskSo quiet it may not register. If you want people to mention the corn, this is not the one.

2. Furrow

A faint set of field rows runs behind the number, with one amber line as the horizon. At full size it is a plowed field seen from the interstate; at 32 px it reads as a clean rule under the 80.

The same rows as a page divider

Where it livesBadge base, section dividers, OG and Instagram card footers.
Why it worksReads as landscape without a single literal object. Ties the mark to the site chrome.
RiskThe amber line can look like a progress bar if it is used on its own. Always pair with the faint rows.

3. Cob Ring

The zero in 80 becomes a ring of kernels, in amber, only at display sizes. At 64 px and below it falls back to the plain 80. The boldest of the three, and the one people will actually notice on an Instagram grid.

Where it livesInstagram avatar, OG card corner, large print. Not the header badge, not the favicon.
Why it worksA cob cross-section is a spiral of kernels — geometric, not a cartoon. It survives a circular crop.
RiskAt small sizes it has to switch to the plain 80, so the mark is not one thing everywhere. More work to maintain.
